# The garden and the late game

Late games die when designer-authored content runs out. The fix is moving the source of goals from the designer to the player and to the systems.

**Early, the garden is where you work. Late, it's what you make.** A genre shift inside one game — from puzzle to composition.

**Give it systemic feedback so it isn't pure decoration.** What you plant determines what shows up: bloom times, scent, night-flowering, and colour attract different moths, bees, and birds. A garden with something in flower every week of the year sustains species a showier garden can't. Now "beautiful" and "alive" are two axes to trade against each other — a composition problem with real depth and no completion state.

**Seasonal shows** replace requests structurally. Procedurally varied criteria (best scented white; must hold form through autumn; judged on uniformity), scored rather than passed. Infinitely generatable, draws on the whole library, and a better score is always possible.

**Seed trading.** Export a specimen as a shareable seed carrying its lineage and its name. Cheap to build, enormously extends the game's life. Someone else's forty-generation blue entering your pool is a genuine event, and brings genes you'd never have bred yourself. Real gardeners have done this forever.

**A note on scope:** it is fine for a cozy game to end. Forty hours, a full notebook, a garden you're proud of, and a natural stopping point is a *good* outcome. The trade-off genetics and the garden will carry a long tail on their own; don't damage the game chasing three hundred hours.
